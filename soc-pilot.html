Can AI Really Run a Security Operations Center?

Why I think the future needs skilled SOC pilots, not just smarter machines.

Can AI Really Run a Security Operations Center?

Imagine working in a security operations center and opening your dashboard to find hundreds of alerts. Some are harmless login mistakes, some are repeated scans, and a few might point to an actual attack. A human analyst cannot give every alert the same amount of attention. That is why the idea of letting artificial intelligence handle routine work sounds useful to me. However, useful is not the same as safe. If AI starts taking action instead of simply giving suggestions, a wrong decision can affect real people and business operations. My view is that the SOC pilot model is realistic as a direction for cybersecurity, but only when organizations keep clear limits on what AI is allowed to do. The goal should be to make analysts more effective, not to make them disappear.

The SOC Pilot Idea: Helpful but Not a Perfect Comparison

In his IBM article, John Velisaris (2025) compares a future security operations center, or SOC, to a modern passenger airplane. Automation handles much of the ordinary work, while a trained pilot steps in when the situation becomes unusual. In a SOC, AI agents could investigate alerts, collect details from different security tools, and carry out approved responses. Human analysts would focus on unexpected threats, such as attacks involving newly discovered vulnerabilities. I understand why this comparison is appealing. Nobody wants skilled analysts to spend an entire shift copying information from one dashboard to another. If AI can reduce that repetitive work, people will have more time to think about serious threats. Still, an airplane follows a carefully tested system with known operating procedures. A company network changes constantly. New devices appear, employees change roles, software gets updated, and attackers deliberately try to fool defenses. This makes cybersecurity a difficult environment for full autonomy. Velisaris also acknowledges that it remains uncertain whether AI will truly put SOC staff in the pilot's chair. I think that uncertainty matters. The model is a promising goal, not proof that human analysts can already leave routine operations unattended.

Where I Would Let AI Work Independently

There are several jobs I would feel comfortable giving to an AI agent, especially if its permissions are limited. It could group duplicate alerts, search for known malicious file hashes, collect related logs, and prepare a timeline of suspicious activity. It could also check whether an endpoint has the latest security updates and create a ticket for an analyst. These tasks save time without immediately changing an important system. Some automatic responses may also be reasonable. For example, if a security tool detects a known malicious file on an ordinary test workstation, an approved rule could quarantine that file. But even this should depend on reliable identification, clear conditions, and a way to reverse the action. I would not give an AI agent unlimited access to every computer just because it usually makes correct choices. Instead, I would start with read-only investigation, test its recommendations against previous incidents, and slowly allow specific low-risk actions. Every automatic decision should be logged so analysts can understand what happened. Speed is valuable, but the ability to explain and undo a decision is just as important.

Decisions That Still Need a Human

I believe people must stay involved whenever a response could cause major damage. Taking a production server offline, disabling an executive's account, blocking a network used by customers, or deleting suspicious files from an important database can interrupt business operations. Even if an alert is genuine, the best response is not always the fastest one. An analyst may need to speak with a system owner, preserve evidence, or decide whether temporary monitoring is safer than immediate shutdown. Human judgment is especially important when a threat is unfamiliar. A zero-day attack may not match existing patterns, and the attacker may be trying to mislead security tools. AI can collect clues and suggest possibilities, but a trained person should evaluate conflicting evidence and make the final call for high-impact actions. There is also an accountability question. If an AI agent shuts down a service and customers lose access, the organization cannot simply blame the software. Someone must have approved the rules, reviewed the risks, and accepted responsibility for the system. For me, a responsible SOC pilot is not someone who watches AI work without understanding it. That person must be ready to challenge the machine and take control.

What Happens When Asset Information Is Wrong?

One of the strongest points in the IBM article is that automation needs context. Velisaris (2025) explains that a response playbook may quarantine a host only if the system knows that it is not a server and that malicious activity has been recognized. This sounds like a small detail, but it can change the outcome of an incident. Imagine an AI agent receives an alert from a device named OFFICE-PC-12. Its inventory says the device is an ordinary employee workstation. The agent decides to isolate it from the network. Unfortunately, the inventory has not been updated. That device is now being used to run a service needed for daily sales. What looked like a routine containment step could interrupt the business. The opposite mistake is also possible. A compromised device might be marked as critical, causing the agent to delay an action that could have stopped an attack. Incomplete information can therefore lead to both unnecessary disruption and missed threats. Another problem is false confidence. An AI system might combine a hostname, IP address, and an old database entry, then produce a convincing explanation that is still wrong. A detailed answer does not guarantee that its source data is correct. This is why an agent should check when records were updated, compare multiple trusted sources, and clearly label uncertain information. When important details conflict or are missing, the safe action is often to pause the disruptive response and ask an analyst to verify the asset. Organizations also need people who maintain accurate inventories. Better AI cannot fully solve a badly managed asset database.

Rules That Make Automation More Responsible

The National Institute of Standards and Technology offers a useful way to think about this problem. Its AI Risk Management Framework describes ongoing work to govern, map, measure, and manage AI risks (Tabassi, 2023). I would apply that idea to an autonomous SOC before allowing agents to take important actions. First, define which assets and situations the AI can handle without approval. Second, test the system with realistic scenarios, including outdated asset records, false alerts, and unusual network behavior. Third, measure not only how quickly the AI responds but also how often it makes harmful or unnecessary changes. Fourth, review those results and change the rules when problems appear. Access control also matters. An agent that only needs to read logs should not have permission to shut down servers. For actions that are more serious, approval should come from a human analyst. I would also want an emergency stop, rollback procedures, audit logs, and regular practice exercises. These safeguards may make the system slightly slower, but they can prevent one bad automated decision from becoming a bigger incident. Responsible autonomy should be earned through testing and monitoring, not assumed because the tool is called intelligent.

What I Need to Learn to Become a SOC Pilot

As an Information Technology student, I do not want to prepare for a career by learning only how to click buttons in a security dashboard. If AI can already collect logs and summarize common alerts, I need to develop skills that help me understand what those results actually mean. Networking is one of my priorities. I should know how devices communicate, how IP addresses and ports work, and how suspicious traffic differs from normal activity. I also need to study operating systems, access permissions, malware behavior, and incident response. Scripting would help me check evidence and build small tools instead of depending completely on automated reports. Another skill is asking good questions. If an AI agent says a workstation is infected, I should be able to ask which evidence supports that conclusion, whether the device identity is reliable, and what could happen if we isolate it. Communication is important too. During an incident, a SOC pilot may need to explain a technical problem to a manager who does not understand cybersecurity terms. Finally, I need to practice making decisions under pressure while respecting privacy and business needs. I see AI as a tool that can help me learn faster, but I should never let it replace my own understanding. To become a SOC pilot, I need to know when to trust automation, when to question it, and when to take responsibility myself.

Final Thoughts

I support the idea of a SOC where AI handles repetitive investigations and helps analysts focus on difficult cases. It could reduce alert fatigue and improve response time, especially for teams that do not have enough people to examine every warning manually. But I do not think minimal human involvement should mean minimal human responsibility. The most dangerous mistakes may happen when an AI agent is confident about an asset it does not actually understand. A responsible organization should limit permissions, verify context, test decisions, and keep people in charge of high-impact responses. For me, the future SOC pilot is not a person being replaced by a machine. It is a person who understands the machine well enough to use it wisely—and to stop it when necessary.
