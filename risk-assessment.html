Risk Assessment Fieldwork Reflection

What Our Risk Assessment Fieldwork Taught Us About Information Security in Practice

September 2026 • Information Security • Risk Assessment

Learning Information Security Beyond the Classroom

Before conducting our risk assessment fieldwork, we mostly understood information security through classroom lessons, discussions, and theoretical examples. We knew that organizations need strong passwords, updated software, secure networks, access controls, employee awareness, and proper procedures. However, conducting an actual risk assessment helped us understand that information security is much more than technology. It also involves people, processes, communication, decision-making, and the way an organization prepares for and responds to problems.

During our fieldwork, our group assessed information security risks within an organization. Because the organization trusted us with information for academic purposes, we understand that confidentiality is part of the responsibility of performing a risk assessment. Therefore, this reflection does not mention specific employees, usernames, accounts, system configurations, or detailed vulnerabilities. Instead, we discuss the findings in general terms and focus on what the experience taught us.

One of the biggest lessons we learned is that risk assessment is about understanding what could go wrong before it becomes a serious problem. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 presents cybersecurity as an ongoing process involving governance, identification, protection, detection, response, and recovery (Pascoe et al., 2024). Seeing these ideas in an actual assessment made the concepts much easier for us to understand.

Instead of simply asking whether an organization has security tools, we started asking more practical questions: What could happen? How likely is it to happen? What would be the impact? What controls already exist? And what can reasonably be done to reduce the risk?

Identifying Risks Is More Than Finding Technical Problems

The first thing our fieldwork taught us was that risks can come from many different sources. At first, we expected our assessment to focus mostly on computers, networks, software, and other technical components. As we worked through the assessment, however, we realized that everyday activities and organizational processes can also create security risks.

For example, equipment or system problems may interrupt normal operations. A device that stops working, an unexpected power problem, or a technical failure may seem like a normal IT issue. However, when employees depend on that equipment to perform important tasks, the problem can also become an information security and operational risk.

We observed that equipment-related risks can be reduced through maintenance, reporting procedures, technical support, replacement of damaged equipment, software updates, and appropriate backups. This made us realize that organizations should not always wait for something to fail before taking action. Preventive maintenance and preparation can reduce disruption and help an organization continue its work.

This taught us that information security also includes availability. Protecting information from unauthorized access is important, but information and systems must also be available to authorized users when they need them.

Access Control Is an Important Part of Security

Another important lesson from our assessment was the importance of controlling who can access information systems and organizational resources. Unauthorized access can potentially lead to the exposure, modification, or misuse of information.

During our assessment, we considered access control from a general security perspective rather than examining or exposing individual accounts. Protecting the confidentiality of those details was important to us. Our group realized that simply having a login system does not automatically mean that access is secure.

Organizations need to make sure that users receive access appropriate to their responsibilities. A person who does not need access to a particular resource should not automatically have it. This helped us better understand the principle of least privilege. Users should have enough access to perform their assigned tasks, but unnecessary permissions should be limited.

We also learned that access control should not be treated as a one-time setup. Employees can change positions, responsibilities can change, and systems can change over time. Because of this, organizations should periodically review user permissions and remove access that is no longer necessary.

Phishing Was One of the Most Important Lessons

Perhaps one of the most important lessons from our fieldwork was the risk of phishing. Phishing is concerning because it targets human behavior. Instead of depending only on a technical vulnerability, an attacker can try to convince a person to click a link, open an attachment, provide information, or perform an action.

Our assessment considered phishing-related risks such as suspicious emails, deceptive links, and messages that may appear to come from legitimate sources. We will not describe the actual messages or specific indicators that were observed because doing so could expose information about the organization.

The broader lesson was clear: employees are an important part of an organization's security defense. This changed the way we think about cybersecurity. Before the assessment, it was easy to think that firewalls, antivirus software, access controls, and other technical protections were the main defenses. After the assessment, we understood that a security system can still be placed at risk when users are not prepared to recognize suspicious activity.

CISA recommends that users learn to recognize and report phishing attempts rather than interacting with suspicious messages. This supports the idea that security awareness should be treated as part of an organization's overall security strategy.

Security Awareness Should Be Continuous

Our assessment also made us realize that cybersecurity awareness should not happen only once. Employees may already know that suspicious emails can be dangerous, but attackers constantly change the way they communicate. A malicious message can look professional, use familiar branding, create urgency, or appear to come from someone the employee knows.

Because of this, security awareness needs to be continuous and practical. One of the treatments we considered appropriate was continuing cybersecurity awareness activities and keeping a clear procedure for reporting suspicious messages.

We believe this is important because employees should feel comfortable reporting suspicious activity. If people are afraid of being blamed for making a mistake, they may delay reporting it. A quick report gives the organization an opportunity to investigate and respond before a small problem becomes a larger incident.

This showed us that security culture matters. Information security should not be viewed as something that belongs only to the IT department. Employees in different roles can contribute to protecting organizational information by following procedures and reporting unusual activity.

Remote Access Also Requires Protection

Another lesson from our risk assessment was the importance of securing remote and network access. Remote access can be useful because employees may need to connect to organizational resources from different locations. However, every additional connection can also introduce additional security considerations.

Secure remote access controls, such as VPN technology, can help protect communications when employees need to access resources remotely. However, using a security technology does not automatically eliminate risk. Security controls must be properly configured, maintained, monitored, and updated.

Credentials also need to be protected, and access should be reviewed when circumstances change. This was an important realization for our group. We learned that security is not about installing one tool and assuming the organization is protected forever. Controls need continuous attention.

Risk Treatment Is About Reducing Risk

Another important part of our fieldwork was learning how to decide what should be done about an identified risk. Finding risks was only one step. We also needed to think about their likelihood, possible impact, existing controls, and the appropriate treatment.

Our assessment considered impact and probability when determining the level of risk. Some risks were considered moderate, while others required more attention because they could have a greater effect on operations or information security.

For risks that needed stronger treatment, our recommendations included improving access reviews, strengthening security awareness, maintaining equipment, improving reporting procedures, and coordinating incident response.

We learned that it is unrealistic to expect an organization to eliminate every possible risk. Instead, the goal is to reduce risk to an acceptable level.

This changed our understanding of risk management. A risk does not necessarily disappear just because a control has been implemented. There may still be residual risk that needs to be monitored and reviewed. For example, employee awareness and email security controls can reduce the likelihood of a successful phishing attack, but they cannot guarantee that every malicious message will be stopped.

Incident Response Is Just as Important as Prevention

Our fieldwork also showed us that prevention is only one part of information security. Even organizations with good security controls may eventually experience an incident. For this reason, organizations need clear procedures for reporting, investigating, containing, and recovering from security incidents.

We recommended maintaining clear incident response procedures and encouraging coordination between the appropriate operational, IT, and cybersecurity personnel. This connects with the Respond and Recover functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework.

As students, this helped us understand why an incident response plan should not simply exist as a document. People need to know what to do, who should be contacted, what information should be recorded, and how communication should happen during an incident.

What We Learned as a Group

Overall, our risk assessment fieldwork changed the way we look at information security. Before the activity, cybersecurity seemed mostly focused on technical solutions. After completing the assessment, we realized that effective information security requires a combination of technology, people, policies, procedures, and continuous improvement.

We learned that a simple equipment problem can become an operational risk. We learned that access control needs regular review. We learned that phishing can exploit human behavior rather than technical weaknesses alone. We also learned that security awareness, incident reporting, and response procedures are just as important as firewalls, VPNs, software updates, and other technical controls.

Most importantly, we learned that risk assessment is not about finding someone to blame. It is about identifying situations that could negatively affect an organization and finding reasonable ways to reduce those risks.

Our experience also taught us the importance of confidentiality. During a real assessment, it can be tempting to include specific examples because they make a report more detailed. However, information security professionals have a responsibility to protect the organization and the people involved.

For this blog, we therefore focused on general lessons instead of exposing names, accounts, specific systems, or detailed vulnerabilities. We believe this is also an important part of professional information security practice.

Conclusion

Our risk assessment fieldwork gave us a much better understanding of what information security looks like in the real world. The activity showed us that cybersecurity is not something that only IT professionals are responsible for. Everyone who uses an organization's systems can contribute to either reducing or increasing risk.

We also learned that organizations should continuously identify risks, protect important assets, detect suspicious activities, respond appropriately to incidents, and recover when problems occur. These ideas are consistent with NIST's cybersecurity guidance, which treats cybersecurity risk management as an ongoing process rather than a one-time activity.

As future IT professionals, this experience reminded us that our role is not simply to fix computers or configure networks. We also need to understand the risks that organizations face and recommend practical controls that fit their needs.

In the end, our biggest takeaway is simple: good information security is not about making an organization completely risk-free. It is about understanding the risks, reducing them responsibly, protecting people and information, and being prepared when something goes wrong.

That is what our risk assessment fieldwork taught us—and it is a lesson we can carry with us as we continue developing our skills in information technology.
