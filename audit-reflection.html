Beyond the Risk Assessment Form: Seeing Information Security Through an Auditor’s Eyes

Asset management, ownership, control evidence, and practical ways to make risk treatment more effective.

Introduction: A Different Way of Looking at Risk

When I first studied risk assessment, I thought the hardest part was identifying threats and deciding how serious they might be. A Risk Assessment Form helps organize that work. It asks us to think about what might go wrong, what could be affected, and what actions could reduce the damage. But looking at the same process through an auditor’s eyes brings up a different question: how do we know that the organization is actually doing what the form says it should do?

For this reflection, I use a small-business scenario to illustrate the kind of questions an auditor would ask. Our group’s actual Risk Assessment Form was not available for this write-up, so the examples below are not verified findings about a specific organization. They are practical audit questions that can guide a review of the real form. This approach also protects confidentiality by avoiding names, locations, internal records, and specific security weaknesses.

An auditor does not simply count the number of recommended controls. The auditor looks for ownership, consistency, and evidence. That difference changed the way I think about information security: a plan is useful, but it must lead to actions that can be checked.

Knowing What the Organization Owns

The first audit question would be simple: does the organization know what assets it depends on? In a small business, important assets might include computers, phones used for work, customer information, financial documents, payment services, stock records, and paper files. Not all of these assets look technical, but each can affect daily operations.

For example, a business phone might be used to communicate with suppliers, receive customer inquiries, or access a payment service. If nobody keeps track of that phone as a business asset, it may be overlooked when employees change responsibilities. The same problem can happen with spreadsheets, notebooks, or shared online accounts.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 includes identifying and managing organizational assets as part of cybersecurity risk management (Pascoe et al., 2024). An auditor would therefore look for an asset inventory rather than rely only on verbal explanations. The inventory should identify the asset, its business purpose, its responsible role, and when its details were last checked.

I realized that recognizing assets during a classroom activity is only a starting point. In a real organization, the list must remain useful after the assessment is finished.

Which Assets Matter Most?

An auditor would also ask which assets are most important to the organization. A device may be inexpensive but still support a critical business process. On the other hand, some equipment can be replaced quickly without seriously interrupting operations. That is why importance should be based on business impact, not just purchase price.

In the small-business example, customer information, payment-related accounts, and financial records would deserve careful attention. Customer records may contain private information. Payment accounts affect the ability to receive money. Financial records support reporting and decisions. Inventory information matters because inaccurate counts can affect ordering, sales, and customer service.

I would evaluate each asset using confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Confidentiality asks who should be allowed to see the information. Integrity asks whether the information stays correct and complete. Availability asks whether it can be used when the business needs it.

These questions help an auditor understand why one asset may need stronger protection than another. They also help management choose controls that fit the actual importance of the asset.

Classification and Ownership

Once assets are identified, the next step is to classify them. Public information, such as general announcements, can be shared openly. Internal information, such as routine operating documents, is intended for the organization. Confidential information, such as private customer details and sensitive financial records, needs stricter handling. The organization should define its own approved categories and make sure staff understand them.

An auditor would check whether classification rules apply to both electronic and paper records. A printed report does not become less sensitive just because it is not stored on a computer. Proper storage, access, retention, and disposal should follow the information’s classification.

Ownership is just as important. Every important asset should have an accountable role. For example, a finance role might oversee financial records, while an administrative role might manage shared business equipment. The owner does not need to perform every task personally, but should know what protections are expected and who carries them out.

I think unclear ownership is one of the easiest problems to overlook. If everybody assumes somebody else is responsible, important reviews may never happen. An auditor would therefore look for a documented assignment of responsibilities, not just an informal understanding.

Proving That Security Controls Work

A Risk Assessment Form may recommend passwords, backups, restricted access, secure storage, regular reviews, and employee awareness. These sound reasonable, but an auditor cannot treat a recommendation as proof that a control works.

Suppose a business says that important files are backed up. An auditor would ask for a backup schedule, records showing whether backups completed, and evidence of a successful restoration test. If the files cannot be restored when needed, the existence of backup software alone does not solve the problem.

The same principle applies to access control. A statement that only authorized workers can open sensitive records should be supported by an approved access list and evidence that permissions are reviewed when duties change. For physical documents, an auditor might review procedures and observe how records are stored.

NIST Special Publication 800-53A provides structured methods for assessing whether security and privacy controls are implemented and operating as intended (Joint Task Force, 2022). This helped me understand that auditing is about evidence, not assumptions. A control is more convincing when the organization can show when it was performed, who checked it, and what happened when a problem was found.

What Evidence Would Be Needed?

If I were preparing an audit, I would start with the organization’s risk assessment and ask what proof supports each important statement. For asset management, I would request an inventory with current ownership and classification. For access control, I would review authorization records and the process for removing access when it is no longer needed.

For backups, I would look for logs and restoration results. For physical security, I would review applicable procedures and make appropriate observations. For employee awareness, I would check whether training or instructions had been provided and whether employees understood their responsibilities. For equipment maintenance, I would look for dated inspection or service records.

Interviews would also help, but they should be compared with other evidence. If someone says a review happens every month, there should ideally be a record of those reviews. An auditor should not assume a control failed simply because one document is missing, but the absence of reliable evidence makes it harder to reach a confident conclusion.

Good evidence should be relevant, current, and sufficient for the question being asked. Collecting many documents is not useful if they do not demonstrate that the specific control is working.

Possible Gaps an Auditor Might Explore

Without the actual Risk Assessment Form and supporting records, I cannot claim that any particular weakness exists. Still, I can identify the areas that an auditor would reasonably examine.

One possible gap is an incomplete inventory. Assets might be known to employees but not recorded in one reliable place. Another is unclear ownership, especially when one person handles several tasks. A third is a lack of documented testing. A backup or access rule might exist, but there may be little evidence that it has been reviewed recently.

An auditor could also check whether policies are updated when the business changes. A procedure written for an older process may no longer match how work is done. Another question is whether accepted risks are clearly approved and reviewed. Accepting a risk should be an informed management decision, not simply leaving an issue unattended.

These possibilities are questions to investigate, not accusations. I learned that an auditor should distinguish between what is documented, what is observed, and what still needs verification. That makes the audit more fair and useful.

How an Asset Inventory Strengthens Risk Treatment

An asset inventory would make the Risk Assessment Form more practical. Each risk could be connected to a particular asset or group of assets, and each asset could have a named responsible role. A simple spreadsheet might contain an asset identifier, description, category, importance, owner role, general location, applicable controls, and last review date.

This would help the organization avoid overlooking assets when planning security improvements. It would also make changes easier to manage. When a device is replaced or an employee changes duties, the inventory can be updated and the relevant controls reviewed.

For example, if a recommendation involves protecting financial records, the inventory can show where those records are managed and which role is accountable. If a recommendation involves business devices, the inventory can help confirm which devices should be checked.

I believe the main benefit is accountability. A recommendation becomes stronger when it is tied to an asset, a responsible role, and a date for review.

Why an Audit Checklist Matters

An audit checklist turns general security goals into repeatable questions. Instead of writing “improve access control,” a checklist could ask: Is the authorized access list current? Who approved it? When was it last reviewed? What evidence supports the answer?

Other checklist questions might ask whether all important assets have owners, whether sensitive records follow the approved classification rules, whether backups have been restored successfully during a test, and whether corrective actions from earlier reviews have been completed.

Each item should identify the evidence expected, the reviewer, the date, the result, and any follow-up action. This is especially useful for a small organization because it creates a routine without requiring an expensive system.

An audit checklist also makes progress visible. If a control was not fully implemented during one review, the organization can assign an action and verify it during the next review. That is better than repeating the same recommendation every time without knowing whether anything improved.

Conclusion: From Recommendations to Evidence

Revisiting risk assessment from an auditor’s perspective made me realize that information security is not finished when a form is submitted. The form identifies risks and suggests treatments, but the organization still needs to manage its assets, assign responsibilities, and demonstrate that its controls work.

The most important assets should be classified according to their sensitivity and business impact. Their owners should be clear. Security measures should be supported by records, observations, and appropriate testing. Possible gaps should be investigated fairly rather than assumed to exist.

An updated asset inventory and a practical audit checklist would strengthen risk treatment because they connect each recommendation to an asset, a responsible role, and evidence of completion. They also make future reviews easier and help the organization improve over time.

As an IT student, my biggest lesson is that good security involves both technology and accountability. Knowing what should be done is important, but being able to show that it was done is what makes a security program trustworthy.
